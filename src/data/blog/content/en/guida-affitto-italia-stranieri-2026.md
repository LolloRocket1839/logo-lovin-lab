# Renting in Italy: complete guide for expats and foreigners (2026)

Everything you need to know about Italian rental contracts, in plain English. The 6 contract types, documents you'll need, your rights as a tenant, deposit rules, average rents, and the traps to avoid.

---

## Before you start: legal requirements

**EU citizens** can rent property in Italy without any special permit. If you stay longer than 3 months, you must register with the local *Anagrafe* (registry office). This doesn't affect your ability to sign a lease.

**Non-EU citizens** need a valid visa or residence permit (*permesso di soggiorno*) to sign a long-term rental contract. A work visa, student visa, elective residence visa, or [digital nomad visa](/blog/torino-digital-nomads-guide) all qualify. For short-term rentals (under 30 days), a tourist visa is sufficient.

Regardless of nationality, every tenant needs a **codice fiscale** — Italy's tax identification number. It's free, takes 10 minutes, and you can get one at any Agenzia delle Entrate office in Italy (bring your passport) or at an Italian consulate abroad before arriving. Without it, you cannot sign any contract lasting more than 30 days.

> **Pro tip: get your codice fiscale before arriving.** Apply at the Italian consulate in your home country. Having it ready when you arrive saves days of bureaucracy and lets you start apartment hunting immediately. The number is generated from your name, date of birth, and nationality — it takes minutes. ## The 6 types of Italian rental contracts

Italian law offers six distinct contract types. The one that's right for you depends on how long you're staying and your situation.

| Contract type | Duration | Rent | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Canone libero (4+4) | 4 + 4 years | Free market | Long-term expats, families |
| Canone concordato (3+2) | 3 + 2 years | Regulated (lower) | Budget-conscious tenants in major cities |
| Transitorio | 1 – 18 months | Free or regulated* | Work assignments, temporary relocations |
| Studenti | 6 – 36 months | Regulated | University students (Erasmus, degree programs) |
| Commerciale (6+6) | 6 + 6 years | Free market | Businesses, offices, shops |
| Locazione breve | 1 – 30 days | Free market | Short visits, house-hunting trips |

*In 11 major metro areas (Rome, Milan, Florence, etc.) the transitorio contract rent must follow local agreements.*

### The 4+4 contract (most common for expats)

This is Italy's standard residential lease. The rent is freely negotiated between you and the landlord. It lasts a minimum of 4 years and automatically renews for another 4 unless one party sends a termination notice at least 6 months before expiry.

You can leave early for serious reasons (job transfer, health, etc.) with 6 months' notice. Many contracts include a "free exit" clause (*recesso libero*) letting you leave anytime with just the notice period. Always check for this before signing.

### The 3+2 contract (lower rent, tax benefits)

The rent is set by local agreements between landlord and tenant associations. It's typically below market rate. In exchange, the landlord gets substantial tax breaks (flat tax of just 10% instead of 21%). As a tenant, you benefit from lower rent and no inflation adjustments. Learn more: [Canone concordato Turin guide](/blog/canone-concordato-torino-2026-guida-completa).

### The transitorio contract (1-18 months)

Perfect for expats on fixed-term work assignments. You need a documented temporary reason — a work contract, employer's letter, or medical need. It ends automatically at the agreed date with no renewal.

### The student contract (6-36 months)

Available only to students enrolled at a university in a different city from their residence. Rent follows local agreements. Can be signed individually or jointly with other students. If you're doing Erasmus in Italy, this is your contract type.

## Documents you'll need to rent

Italian landlords are cautious and expect thorough documentation. Having everything ready before you start viewing apartments gives you a significant advantage — especially in competitive markets like Milan and Rome.

**Essential documents (everyone):**
- Valid passport or national ID card
- Codice fiscale (Italian tax code)
- Proof of income (employment contract, last 3 pay slips, or bank statements)
- Contact email and Italian phone number

**For non-EU citizens, additionally:**
- Valid visa or *permesso di soggiorno* (residence permit)
- Or receipt of your permit application (*ricevuta* — accepted by most landlords while the permit is being processed)

**Strongly recommended:**
- Italian bank account (IBAN) — many landlords require rent payments via Italian bank transfer. Some refuse foreign bank transfers due to delays and fees. Read our guide: [Bank account for international students](/blog/conto-corrente-studenti-stranieri-italia-2026).

> **Checklist before your first viewing:** Passport/ID copy, codice fiscale, proof of income (3 months), Italian phone number, Italian IBAN (or proof of account opening), and for non-EU: visa/permesso. Having these ready signals to landlords that you're a serious, organized tenant.

## Costs breakdown: what you'll actually pay

Beyond the monthly rent, there are several costs to budget for when signing an Italian lease.

**Security deposit (deposito cauzionale):** legally capped at 3 months' rent. Paid at signing, returned at the end of the lease (plus accrued legal interest) after the landlord verifies the property's condition. Cannot be used to cover your last months' rent.

**Agency fee (provvigione):** if you use a real estate agent, expect 1 month's rent + VAT (22%) as a one-time commission. Some agents charge both landlord and tenant. Always clarify fees upfront.

**Registration costs:** the contract must be registered with the tax authority (Agenzia delle Entrate) within 30 days. Under the standard tax regime, you split the registration tax with the landlord (2% of annual rent, minimum €67, plus stamp duty). Under [cedolare secca](/blog/cedolare-secca-2026-investitori): no registration tax or stamp duty.

**Building fees (spese condominiali):** typically €50-150/month. Covers cleaning, elevator, lighting, building insurance.

**Utilities (utenze):** electricity, gas, water, internet, and waste tax (TARI). Budget approximately €100-200/month depending on apartment size and season. Check our [student budget calculator](/tools/budget-calculator) for personalized estimates.

## Your rights as a tenant

Italian law strongly protects tenants. Here are the rights you should know about.

**Your landlord cannot evict you without cause.** During the first 4 years (in a 4+4 contract), the landlord can only refuse renewal for specific legal reasons: personal use, sale, or major renovation.

**Your deposit must earn interest.** The landlord must pay you legal interest on your security deposit every year.

**The contract must be registered.** An unregistered contract is fiscally void. If your landlord refuses to register, you can report it to the Agenzia delle Entrate — which can result in the rent being reduced to the regulated rate.

**Rent increases are limited.** Under a standard contract, rent can only increase by the ISTAT inflation index (and only if the contract includes this clause). Under cedolare secca, no increase is possible for the entire duration.

**You have the right to a written contract.** Verbal rental agreements have no legal force in Italy. If a landlord proposes an informal arrangement, walk away.

## What is cedolare secca (and why it matters to you)

The *cedolare secca* is a flat tax regime the landlord can opt for instead of standard income taxation. It has two major benefits for tenants:

**Benefit 1: Frozen rent.** The landlord cannot increase your rent based on inflation (ISTAT index) for the entire contract duration. In a period of 3-5% annual inflation, this saves you hundreds of euros per year.

**Benefit 2: No registration fees.** Under the standard regime, you split the registration tax (2% of annual rent) with the landlord. Under cedolare secca: no registration tax and no stamp duty.

The rate is 21% for standard contracts (4+4) and 10% for regulated-rent contracts (3+2, student, transitorio in major cities). Since 10% is very attractive, landlords in major cities often offer 3+2 contracts at below-market rent — a win-win.

> **Ask about cedolare secca when negotiating.** Many landlords are willing — especially for reliable tenants. It locks your rent, eliminates registration fees, and usually makes the landlord better off too (21% flat vs. 33-43% income tax). ## How to terminate your contract

As a tenant, you can leave your Italian rental in three ways.

**At the natural expiry:** send a registered letter (*raccomandata A/R*) or certified email (PEC) to your landlord at least 6 months before the contract expires. No reason needed.

**Early termination for serious reasons (*gravi motivi*):** you can leave at any time if you have a documented serious reason — job transfer, health emergency, family emergency. Send the registered letter with 6 months' notice and clearly state the reason.

**With a free exit clause (*recesso libero*):** if your contract includes this clause, you can leave with just the agreed notice period (usually 3-6 months) without needing a reason. Check your contract carefully.

In all cases, you must pay rent through the entire notice period, even if you leave the property earlier.

## Five traps to avoid

**Trap 1: "Cash only" arrangements.** Some landlords propose unregistered contracts paid in cash. This is illegal (*affitto in nero*), leaves you without legal protection, and exposes both parties to severe fines (120-240% of unpaid tax). Always insist on a registered written contract.

**Trap 2: Deposit beyond 3 months.** Any request for more than 3 months' rent as a security deposit is illegal under Italian law. If a landlord asks for more — it's a red flag. Cite Art. 11 of L. 392/1978.

**Trap 3: No APE attached.** The Energy Performance Certificate must be attached to every rental contract. If it's missing, the landlord faces fines of €1,000-18,000.

**Trap 4: Skipping the handover report.** Always insist on a *verbale di consegna* — a signed document describing the exact condition of the apartment when you move in. Take dated photos of everything.

**Trap 5: Paying the landlord's share of registration.** Under Italian law, registration costs are split 50/50. Any contract clause making the tenant pay 100% is void. Cite Art. 8, L. 392/1978.
