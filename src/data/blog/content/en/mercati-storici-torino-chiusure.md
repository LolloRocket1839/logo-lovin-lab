# Turin's Historic Markets: Closures and Unanalyzed Social Impact

## An Unprecedented Crisis in the Heart of the Working-Class City

Turin is experiencing a crucial and little-known moment in its urban history: the systemic crisis of neighborhood markets, spaces that have represented for over a century the beating heart of neighborhood sociality, collective memory, the place where communities met daily and transmitted values, traditions and relationships. This crisis is not a simple commercial issue, but represents a real anthropological earthquake affecting tens of thousands of people, particularly the elderly, poor families and the most fragile communities. Yet, despite the magnitude of the phenomena characterizing this transformation, the online public narrative remains extraordinarily superficial, fragmentary and unable to grasp the depth of the social impact of these closures.

The Piazza Crispi market, a living symbol of the Barriera di Milano neighborhood and Turin's working-class memory, was definitively suppressed by the Municipality in 2023, after years of slow agony. But Piazza Crispi is only the most evident manifestation of a much broader and systemic crisis: about 30 city markets are now at risk, thousands of street vendors and their families are losing their main income, and the CISM Consortium (Consortium for Market Services Infrastructure) entered judicial liquidation in January 2025 with a debt of 900,000 euros accumulated for unpaid bills, risking leaving hundreds of stalls without electricity and water.

## The History of Turin's Markets: From Industrialization to Deindustrialization

To fully understand the scope of today's crisis, we need to take a step back and trace the history of the neighborhoods that saw the birth and prosperity of Turin's historic markets. The Piazza Crispi market arose in the **Barriera di Milano** neighborhood, an area with roots dating back to 1853, when the first customs belt was created to control goods entering from the north. In this area, the **most important customs gate was the one facing Milan**, from which the neighborhood took its evocative name: Barriera di Milano.

Starting from the late nineteenth century, the Barriera di Milano neighborhood became one of the main industrial hubs of the northern Turin area, as an alternative and complement to the southern area where the large Fiat factories stood. Famous factories were born: **Fiat Grandi Motori** (OGM, founded in 1905), the **CEAT** tire factory (opened in 1939), **textile industries** including Fratelli Piacenza, the **Wamar** biscuit factory on corso Vigevano (founded in 1922), and various other manufacturing realities.

These factories formed the socio-economic fabric around which a densely populated community developed, where workers and their families constituted the living body of the neighborhood. The neighborhood market was not a simple commercial area, but the heart of the social fabric: it was the place where the women and men of the neighborhood met daily, where local memory condensed, where culinary and domestic knowledge was transmitted between generations, where the bonds of solidarity that characterized working-class life were woven.

However, starting from the 1970s-80s, all this began to disintegrate. Progressive deindustrialization emptied the territory of tens of thousands of jobs. The **Officine Grandi Motori (OGM)**, which were the main employer in the Barriera di Milano neighborhood, gradually closed their plants, causing the depopulation of the area. In recent years, the phenomenon has worsened with the gentrification of some areas and the outward movement of population groups previously residing in the territory.

It is in this context of urban transformation without planning that the Piazza Crispi market entered an agony that characterized the 2010-2020 decades. In 2022-2023, the average number of vendors present fell to dramatic levels: **only two stalls on Wednesday, six on Friday**, with fewer and fewer customers who were increasingly elderly. The causes were multiple: neighborhood decay, increase in foreign population (up to 40-42% in some areas) which had modified consumption and commercial habits, competition from large-scale distribution, loss of the critical mass of residents that guaranteed the market's vitality.

## The Unnarrated Social Impact: The Elderly, Street Vendors, Fragile Communities

And this is where the **most serious information gap** emerges: no online analysis seriously addressed the crucial question: **who is affected by these closures?** The answer is complex and would concern tens of thousands of people.

### The Elderly: From Urban Marginality to Total Isolation

Research from the Sociology department of the University of Bicocca in Milan showed that **for one in five elderly people living in neighborhoods without proximity shops, quality of life drastically worsens**. In the northern Turin territory, where neighborhoods with the most fragile elderly population and greatest urban isolation are concentrated (Aurora, Barriera di Milano, Regio Parco), the elderly regularly relied on neighborhood markets for at least three fundamental reasons:

1. **Economic accessibility**: neighborhood markets offer significantly lower prices than supermarkets, crucial for those living on a minimum pension
2. **Geographic proximity**: while supermarkets require movements that for over-75s are often impossible, the neighborhood market near home is reachable even on foot
3. **Social and relational function**: **three out of five elderly people regularly visited neighborhood markets not only to shop, but as a recurring moment of socialization**, human contact, conversation with vendors and other customers

When the neighborhood market closes, for the elderly it's not just "a shop" that closes: it closes the **structuring place of their day**, the fixed appointment around which they organized their relationships, their spatial and temporal orientation, their motivation to leave home.

### Street Vendors: Loss of Income and Dignity

Parallel to the suffering of elderly customers, the street vendors themselves of Turin's markets were experiencing an economic and psychological drama no less serious. For many Turin street vendors (about 60% according to Anva-Confesercenti estimates), the market represented the **only available income**, the activity inherited from parents or grandparents, the work they had learned since childhood.

The consequences of closure were devastating:
- **Immediate impoverishment**: for families living thanks to market income, closure meant immediate loss of 70-100% of their monthly income
- **Shift toward the underground economy**: many vendors, unable to seek new jobs, resorted to illegal trade
- **Loss of identity**: for many, the market was inseparable from their personal and family identity
- **Family cascade effects**: wives, children, any employees saw their economic and professional horizon suddenly downsize

## Toward a Possible Rewriting of the Narrative

The crisis of Turin's markets represents a crucial moment of possible **definitive rupture of social ties, urban memory, community cohesion**. But it also represents an opportunity: the opportunity to recognize the extraordinary value of these spaces, to document their memory, to invent new forms of regeneration that maintain their social and economic function rather than canceling them.

This doesn't require enormous investments, but essentially requires a **rewriting of the narrative** about the city: moving from a vision of Turin that celebrates the "new" and "urban transformation" to one that values resilience, continuity, memory, community cohesion.
