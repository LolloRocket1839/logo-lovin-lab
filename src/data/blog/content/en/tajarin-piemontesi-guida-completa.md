# Piedmontese tajarin: the egg pasta that embodies centuries of tradition

Tajarin represent one of the most authentic and fascinating expressions of Piedmontese cuisine, a fresh egg pasta that incorporates the values of simplicity, tradition, and richness of local products that characterize the region. Born in the gentle hills of the Langhe and Monferrato during the **15th century**, tajarin are long, thin, and distinguished by an **intense yellow color** that makes them immediately recognizable. This particular golden hue is no accident, but rather the direct result of the extraordinarily generous use of egg yolks in the recipe: the traditional formula calls for **30 yolks per kilogram of flour**, a proportion that makes these tagliolini anything but light, yet rich in nutrients and flavor.[1][2][3][4]

Today, at a historic moment when Italian cuisine has been recognized as a **UNESCO World Heritage Site in December 2025**, tajarin occupy a central role in celebrating Piedmontese gastronomic tradition, representing the excellence of Italian fresh pasta and embodying values of sustainability and respect for local products.

## A fascinating history: from medieval origins to modern diffusion

### 15th century: origins in the Langhe of Piedmont

The history of tajarin has its roots in **15th-century Piedmont**, a period when the first documented evidence of this fresh pasta began to appear in culinary records and chronicles of the region. Contrary to what one might think, tajarin did not arise from a quest for abstract culinary refinement, but from the concrete needs of rural Piedmontese cooking, from the **farmhouses of the Langhe and Monferrato**. In this context, flour and eggs were ingredients available on local farms, making tajarin a natural dish to prepare in rural communities.[1][5][3]

However, tajarin were not a daily dish. They were instead reserved for **special occasions, holidays, and important celebrations**, such as weddings, baptisms, and of course Sundays. This association with festivities is closely tied to the richness of the recipe itself: the considerable number of egg yolks required by the traditional formula represented a luxury, an abundance that was only indulged on special days.[2][3][1]

### Vittorio Emanuele II and the "comodino": the royal legend

The history of tajarin intertwines fascinatingly with the history of the Savoy Court through the figure of **King Vittorio Emanuele II**, one of the greatest admirers of this simple Piedmontese dish. According to chronicles handed down and testimonies of oral tradition, Vittorio Emanuele II greatly appreciated tajarin, especially when accompanied by the traditional sauce known as **"comodino"**.[6][7][8]

The term "comodino" derives from the Piedmontese expression "ben comodà," which literally means **"well arranged,"** and represents an extraordinarily caloric and complex ragù composed of lard, sage, rosemary, and farmyard offal (chicken and rabbit livers, combs, kidneys, hearts). Legend has it that the king appreciated this dish so intensely that, to avoid staining his uniform during the meal, he would have his chest covered with a specially attached napkin.[7][9][1][6]

A particularly fascinating role in this story is played by the **Countess of Mirafiori, Rosa Vercellana** (known as "bela Rosin"), the king's morganatic wife and lover. According to traditions passed down, it was the countess herself who prepared tajarin with her own hands for the sovereign, a gesture that testifies to how this dish, although of rural origin, had been elevated to the status of a royal delicacy.[9][7]

## Morphology and distinctive characteristics

### Dimensions: between angel hair and tagliatelle

From a morphological standpoint, tajarin occupy a **fascinating intermediate position** in the hierarchy of Italian egg pastas. They are significantly thinner than traditional tagliatelle, yet at the same time thicker than angel hair. The traditional cut width is around **3-4 millimeters**, and the thickness should not exceed **one millimeter**.[5][10][3][1]

This extraordinary fineness represents both an artistic expression and a technical imperative. Artistically, the thinness of tajarin testifies to the skill and dedication of the artisan who prepares them; technically, the fineness of the cut guarantees an **extremely fast cooking time**, of only 2-3 minutes, while preserving the delicate consistency of the pasta and the complexity of flavors.[10][11]

### The intense yellow color: the imprint of egg yolks

An immediately visually recognizable characteristic of tajarin remains their **extraordinary intense and rich yellow color**. This golden hue is not the result of artificial colorings or chemical agents, but simply the direct consequence of the abundant use of egg yolks in the recipe.[12][2][10][1]

According to the most traditional and authentic formula, for every kilogram of flour, **30 yolks** are used. The yolks give the pasta not only the characteristic rich yellow color, but also an **extraordinary richness** of flavor and a particular softness that radically distinguishes it from any other Italian egg pasta.[4][2][10]

### The ultra-thin sheet: an exercise in artisanal mastery

A crucial characteristic in the preparation of tajarin concerns the fact that the **dough sheet must be extremely thin** — described in various recipes as almost transparent. This thinness is not merely aesthetic, but represents a central element in preserving the dish's gustatory quality. A thin sheet ensures that the pasta cooks quickly, maintaining its al dente texture and preserving the egg flavors that characterize the dish.[11][13]

The traditional technique for obtaining this ultra-thin sheet involves using the **mattarello**, the traditional Piedmontese wooden rolling pin, with which the dough is stretched until reaching a thickness no greater than **two millimeters**. This process requires a considerable amount of strength, patience, and technical skill.[14][8][11]

## The dough: traditional recipe and regional variations

### The classic formula: 30 yolks per kilogram of flour

The traditional and most authentic tajarin recipe represents a fascinating exercise in proportions. To prepare tajarin according to the purest formula of tradition, you need:[2][3][4]

- **1 kilogram of flour** (preferably a mixture of 00 flour and re-milled durum wheat semolina)
- **30 fresh egg yolks**
- **A pinch of salt**
- **An optional tablespoon of white wine**

The reason why the recipe calls for only yolks rather than whole eggs remains central to the philosophy of tajarin. The use of yolks alone gives that extraordinary richness of color and flavor that characterizes the dish.[14]

### The addition of corn flour and semolina

A fascinating detail in tajarin preparation concerns the **addition of corn flour** to the dough. According to some traditional recipes, after rolling out the sheet, it is dusted with **"meliga"** (the Piedmontese dialect name for corn flour), before proceeding with the cut. This addition doesn't directly affect the dough, but serves to separate the layers of pasta and facilitate hand cutting.[16][8]

Similarly, the use of a **mixture of 00 flour and re-milled durum wheat semolina** in the dough represents a conscious technical choice that gives the pasta better hold during cooking.[15][14]

## Preparation technique: from rolling pin to hand cutting

### Rolling out the sheet: the art of the rolling pin

Once the dough has rested sufficiently, it is removed from the refrigerator and rolled out with the **rolling pin** on a board lightly dusted with semolina. The traditional technique involves rotating the sheet clockwise while continuing to pull it with the rolling pin, a rhythmic movement that ensures uniform thickness and a progressively circular shape.[11][14]

The rolled sheet must reach an **extremely thin thickness**, no more than **2 millimeters**, so that it's almost possible to glimpse the work surface beneath. Once this thickness is reached, the sheet is allowed to rest briefly (about 10 minutes) before cutting.[8][11][14]

### Hand cutting: the art of "tajarin"

Here lies the true magic and technical difficulty of tajarin preparation. The sheet is **rolled up on itself** to create a sort of very long, tapered "salami." At this point, using an **extremely sharp knife**, you proceed to cut the roll of pasta as thinly as possible, creating very thin threads.[8][14]

This operation requires an **expert and fast hand**, extraordinary coordination, and a certain physical strength. Piedmontese tradition holds that cutting was entrusted to the most capable men of the house, while women prepared the dough — a division of labor that reflected the physical abilities required for each task.[7][14]

## Condiments: from the simplicity of tradition to contemporary refinement

### Comodino: the traditional Langhe ragù

The **"comodino"** represents the most authentic and celebrated condiment for tajarin, an extraordinarily complex and rich ragù that embodies the philosophy of Piedmontese cuisine. This condiment is not a ragù in the traditional sense, but a sophisticated combination of ingredients.[1][6][8]

The preparation of comodino begins with sautéing a mix of **oil, butter, lard**, along with a very fine mince of **celery, carrot, onion, garlic, parsley, sage, and rosemary**. Once the soffritto has slightly wilted, **chicken and rabbit livers** are added, along with other offal such as combs, kidneys, and hearts. This mix is briefly seared over high heat, then **dried mushrooms** and **crumbled sausage** are added.[1][6]

The final phase of comodino preparation involves adding **red wine** and **tomato sauce**, followed by slow cooking that must last **at least 2 hours** for all the flavors to blend completely.[6][1]

### Tajarin with white truffle from Alba: the noble pairing

If comodino represents popular and rural tradition, **Alba white truffle** represents the pinnacle of refinement in tajarin condiments. This pairing conceals a profound gastronomic logic: the richness of the truffle pairs perfectly with the richness of the egg pasta, creating a dish of extraordinary complexity and elegance.[4][12][11]

The simplest and most fascinating recipe calls for dressing cooked tajarin with **melted butter**, **grated Parmigiano Reggiano**, and completing the dish with **thin shavings of Alba white truffle**. In this extremely simple version, the delicacy of the pasta, the creaminess of the butter and cheese, and the unmistakable aroma of truffle create a symphony of flavors that represents one of the culminating moments of Piedmontese cuisine.[12][4][11]

### Tajarin with roast gravy

Another famous and authentically Piedmontese variant calls for dressing tajarin with **roast gravy**, the rich drippings that form during oven-roasted meat cooking. This condiment represents an expression of Piedmontese home cooking, where the Sunday roast gravy is not discarded but intelligently reused to flavor pasta.[9]

## The authentic recipe: how to prepare tajarin at home

### Ingredients for 4 people

To prepare tajarin following tradition, you need:[13][11][4]

- **500 grams of type 00 flour**
- **50 grams of re-milled durum wheat semolina flour**
- **15 egg yolks** (proportionally reduced from the 30 of the classic recipe)
- **25 milliliters of water**
- **Salt to taste**

Optional but traditional:
- **One tablespoon of extra virgin olive oil**
- **A splash of white wine**

### Step-by-step procedure

**1. Preparing the dough**

Arrange the sifted flour and semolina on a wooden board, forming a well with a hole in the center. Pour the egg yolks, water, and salt into the hole, and begin to quickly beat the yolks with a fork, gradually incorporating flour from the edges of the well.[13][11]

**2. Hand kneading**

Once the yolks are roughly combined with the flour, proceed to knead vigorously by hand, pressing the dough with your palms and continuously folding it over itself. The dough should become progressively smooth, elastic, and an intense yellow color.[11][14]

**3. Initial rest**

Wrap the dough in plastic wrap and let it rest for **20-30 minutes** at room temperature, or preferably for **2 hours** in the refrigerator.[15][14][11]

**4. Rolling out the sheet**

Remove the dough from the refrigerator half an hour before use. Proceed to roll out the dough with the rolling pin on a surface lightly dusted with semolina, rotating the sheet clockwise and progressively stretching it until reaching a thickness of about **2 millimeters**.[14][11]

**5. Resting the sheet**

Let the sheet rest for about **10 minutes** before cutting.[8]

**6. Hand cutting**

Gently roll the sheet onto itself, creating a sort of very long "salami." Using an extremely sharp knife, proceed to cut the roll with quick, decisive movements, creating very thin threads about **3-4 millimeters** wide.[8][14]

**7. Unrolling and drying**

Gently unroll the cut tajarin and place them on a tray covered with a towel dusted with semolina, where they are allowed to dry slightly for **10-15 minutes** before cooking.[8]

**8. Cooking**

Bring plenty of salted water to a boil (about 1 liter for every 100 grams of pasta). Drop the tajarin into the water and cook for about **2-3 minutes**. The pasta is ready when it floats to the surface.[13][11]

**9. Dressing**

Drain the tajarin, preserving a splash of cooking water. Quickly toss in a pan with butter and a tablespoon of cooking water. Transfer to plates and dress with grated Parmigiano Reggiano and, if desired, shavings of Alba white truffle.[13][11]

## Where to taste authentic tajarin: restaurants, trattorias, and pastry shops

The search for authentic tajarin represents a gastronomic adventure worth undertaking with dedication. In Turin and the surrounding Piedmontese territories, you can find a multitude of experiences ranging from classic trattorias that keep popular tradition alive to Michelin-starred restaurants where tajarin are reinterpreted in a contemporary key.

### Turin: heart of the tajarin tradition

**Ristorante Del Cambio** represents the pinnacle of the historical tajarin tradition in Turin. Founded in **1757**, Del Cambio is the most celebrated and famous restaurant in the city, frequented since the Risorgimento by historical figures such as Camillo Benso di Cavour. The restaurant is located in a building adjacent to the Teatro Carignano and remains an institution of Turin gastronomy. On the Del Cambio menu, tajarin maintain a privileged position, prepared strictly by hand according to historical recipes handed down from Piedmontese cookbooks like "Il Cuoco piemontese perfezionato a Parigi" from 1766.[19][20]

**Cianci** represents a typical Piedmontese piola located in the heart of the **Quadrilatero Romano**, open 7 days a week and offering three options for traditional first courses including **tajarin with rabbit ragù**. This venue embodies the genuine style of the Piedmontese table, with a simple culinary offering that is extremely faithful to tradition.[21]

**Cà Dij Mat** is a Piedmontese osteria located in the center of the city offering a strictly traditional menu. The venue is particularly famous for **tajarin with porcini mushrooms** and for the fact that all pasta is prepared in-house, guaranteeing extraordinary authenticity and freshness.[21]

**Ristorante Solferino**, located in **Piazza Solferino** in central Turin, represents a reference point for contemporary Piedmontese cuisine. The venue offers **tajarin with porcini mushrooms** within a menu that celebrates the most iconic dishes of tradition, to be paired with a wise selection from their wine cellar.[22][21]

**Ristorante Consorzio**, located at **Via Monte di Pietà, 23**, offers a wide range of Piedmontese dishes in which tajarin occupy a central position. The venue offers both an à la carte menu and tasting menus with two appetizers, a first course, a main course, and dessert.[21]

**Trattoria Amicizia** offers a classic Piedmontese menu where you can find **tajarin with ragù**, accompanied by other iconic dishes such as risotto with broccoli cream and pork fillet with mustard and apples.[21]

**Tre Galline**, located in the **Quadrilatero**, represents one of the city's first restaurants, with documentary evidence attesting to the existence of an inn of the same name as early as the **18th century**. The venue is particularly famous for braised agnolotti and vitello tonnato, but fresh pasta like tajarin also remains an integral part of the culinary offering.[21]

**Trattoria sotto la Luna** represents a simple and welcoming venue where you can enjoy genuine and seasonal dishes from Piedmontese culinary tradition. The menu is renewed monthly to follow the seasonality of raw materials.[21]

### Alba and the Langhe: the temple of truffle tajarin

**Piazza Duomo Alba** represents the pinnacle of contemporary tajarin interpretation. This restaurant, led by **chef Enrico Crippa**, a true icon of Italian haute cuisine, is located in the heart of Alba and has been awarded **three Michelin Stars**. Within this extraordinary gastronomic experience, the agnolotti del plin with three-meat roast gravy (pork, beef, and rabbit) represent the most famous dish. Enrico Crippa's philosophy is based on research, technique, and absolute respect for raw materials. The restaurant has been president of the Bocuse d'Or jury since 2012 and has been included among the 50 Best Restaurants in the world since 2013.[23][24][25]

**L'Inedito Vigin Mudest** at **via Vernazza 11** represents one of Alba's historic restaurants with an extraordinarily familiar and tranquil atmosphere. The venue offers Alba cuisine in all its authenticity, where tajarin remain absolute protagonists of the menu.[26]

**Aldente**, a small welcoming restaurant located at **via Pertinace, 1**, represents another Alba excellence where **raviolini del plin** and **tajarin** remain central dishes of the culinary offering, often accompanied by Alba white truffle.[26]

**Osteria dell'Arco**, an Alba restaurant specializing in Langhe tradition, is particularly famous for **tajarin dressed with white truffle**. Visitors have reported the truffle eaten on tajarin as particularly memorable.[27]

### Around Alba: farmhouses, hills, and tradition

**Locanda dei Vagabondi** in **Corneliano d'Alba** (via Ruata 6) is a restaurant located in the courtyard of a completely restored old farmhouse, particularly pleasant for dining outdoors in summer. The menu is affordable and substantial, with tajarin and raviolini del plin among the protagonists.[26]

**Ostu d'Racunis** in **Pocapaglia** (Borgata San Martino 7) offers, in addition to **tajarin** (called "tajerin" in local dialect) and raviolini del plin, also excellent homemade desserts such as bunnet (traditional Piedmontese chocolate dessert) and peaches with amaretti and chocolate.[26]

**Trattoria in Piazza** in **Montelupo Albese** (Via Ballerina, 15) is located on the square from which you can enjoy a splendid view of the surrounding Langhe hills. The menu includes typical Piedmontese cuisine dishes, in some cases reinterpreted with contemporary creativity.[26]

**Osteria della Pace** in **Santa Vittoria d'Alba** offers typical Alba cuisine where tajarin remain a central dish, accompanied by other specialties such as Alba-style raw meat and rabbit.[26]

**La Trifula Bianca** in **Borbore di Vezza d'Alba** is known especially for mushrooms but offers many typical specialties from Langhe and Roero, where tajarin find a perfect pairing.[26]

### Monferrato and Asti: peasant tradition

**Trattoria Fratelli Bruzzone** in Monferrato offers **tajarin alla monferrina**, an extraordinarily authentic version where the ragù uses three different meats cooked slowly for many hours, with the final addition of rabbit livers. This represents the most genuine and popular Monferrato tradition.[28]

The **Marengo** and the cuisine of the **Asti** area remain famous for tajarin prepared according to local traditions, often dressed with traditional Langhe ragù or with the offal comodino.[3]

### Pastry shops and retail outlets: where to buy fresh tajarin

For those who would like to take fresh tajarin home:

**Pastificio Bon Aptit** in **Corneliano d'Alba** (via Maestri Cestai 1) offers **raviolini del plin** and **tajarin** prepared according to tradition, with the possibility of purchasing fresh pasta to prepare at home.[26]

**Pastè**, an artisan pasta factory from the Cuneo area, produces tajarin that faithfully reflect the historic Langhe recipe using only local ingredients: durum wheat semolina and fresh Italian eggs. Products are available through specialized e-commerce platforms like Cortilia.[29]

**Grissineria Cravero** in **Barolo** artisanally produces **tajarin, tagliatelle, pappardelle, and maltagliati** according to the ancient Piedmontese recipe, using durum wheat semolina and eggs, with the option to choose variants with black truffle, white truffle, or Barolo wine.[30]

**Savurè** in **Turin** (with retail locations in the San Salvario neighborhood) offers **tajarin with 24 yolks** prepared according to the traditional recipe. The recommended portion is 180 grams per person, with a cooking time of 2 minutes.[31]

### The Alba White Truffle Fair

For a complete gastronomic experience, visit the **International Alba White Truffle Fair**, held every year from October to December. Here you can taste truffle tajarin prepared by the best chefs of the territory, accompanied by the great wines of the Langhe: Barolo, Barbaresco, Nebbiolo.[12]

In **Alba**, at **Tartufi and Co**, you can find not only tajarin but also an extraordinary variety of typical products from Langhe and Roero, including **truffle butter** perfect for dressing tajarin. **TartufLanghe** in **Piobesi d'Alba** (in the Catena Rossa locality) offers typical Langhe specialties where truffle butter represents the ideal accompaniment.[26]

### Suggestions for the complete experience

To experience the complete Piedmontese tajarin tradition, we recommend visiting in sequence: first one of the traditional Turin restaurants (such as Cianci or Cà Dij Mat) to understand the popular tradition, then one of the starred restaurants in Alba (Piazza Duomo) to discover the contemporary interpretation, and finally one of the Monferrato trattorias (such as Fratelli Bruzzone) to savor the most authentic version of peasant cooking.

## Cultural significance and UNESCO recognition

### The inscription of Italian cuisine as UNESCO Heritage (2025)

In **December 2025**, Italian cuisine was officially inscribed on the **UNESCO World Heritage List**, an extraordinary recognition that celebrates the cultural and historical richness of Italian gastronomic tradition in the global context. Within this recognition, Piedmont played a particularly significant role, with tajarin and other Piedmontese stuffed pastas playing a central part.[17][18]

Tajarin, although they may seem like a simple egg pasta, actually represent a compendium of important gastronomic principles that contributed to UNESCO recognition: sustainability, intelligent use of ingredients, the value of artisanal tradition kept alive over the centuries, and the ability to elevate simple ingredients to extraordinary creations.[18]

---

## Conclusion: tajarin as a symbol of Piedmontese excellence

Piedmontese tajarin represent much more than a simple first course. They are living testimonies of a gastronomic tradition that has its roots in the Middle Ages, that has managed to conquer royal tables, and that continues to thrive in contemporary restaurants and family kitchens throughout the region.[3][12][1]

From the extraordinary richness of 30 yolks per kilogram of flour, to the delicate thinness of the sheet, from the tradition of hand cutting to the refinement of pairing with Alba white truffle, every element of the tajarin recipe tells a story of artisanal dedication, deep local culture, and a community that has known how to preserve and transmit generation after generation one of the most fascinating dishes of Italian cuisine.[3][12][1][2]

With the recognition of Italian cuisine as a World Heritage Site in 2025, Piedmontese tajarin have received the international recognition they deserved.
