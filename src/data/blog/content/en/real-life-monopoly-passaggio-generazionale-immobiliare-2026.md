# Real life Monopoly: €2.72 trillion of bricks about to change hands {#intro}

*Why the largest real-estate redistribution in Italian history has already begun — and what it means for those who know how to put a home back to work.*

---

## The number that redraws the map {#data}

On 20 May 2026 *Il Sole 24 Ore* published a snapshot worth reading carefully: today roughly **half of Italian homes are owned by people around seventy**, and over the coming years a property portfolio estimated at **€2.72 trillion** will enter a phase of generational transfer. Not just any homes: largely **large apartments located in major cities**.

It's not an isolated figure. The *UBS Global Wealth Report 2025* estimates that over the next twenty years more than €2.3 trillion will pass from one generation to the next in Italy, and that **more than 60% of that value will be in real estate**. The projections all converge on one point: **by 2045 roughly one home out of two will change owner** through inheritance or donation.

In other words, the board is about to tip. And as in any game, what matters is what you do with the squares that open up.

---

## The problem: frozen wealth {#problem}

There's a second number, less cited but decisive: an estimated **27% of Italian real estate stock is empty or underused**. Often large apartments in valuable urban locations, but idle — not lived in, not rented, not maintained.

The reason is structural. Because people live longer, inheritance now arrives late: those who receive a home are often already adults or seniors themselves, with their own home and a life set up elsewhere. The result is predictable. According to market data, after an inheritance **roughly 35% of properties are sold, 30% simply held, 20% rented out and 15% split among family members**.

That 35% sold, and a good share of the 30% "held" without a plan, is the knot: homes leaving the hands of those who don't use them, but which don't always find someone who knows how to put them back to work. It's capital — literally — locked in place. The economist Thomas Piketty observed that the more a country's wealth is transferred by inheritance, the more growth slows down: the value exists, but it doesn't work.

---

## The same problem, seen from the other side {#turin}

While thousands of apartments stay empty, in university cities the opposite happens. In Turin more than 40,000 out-of-town students are looking for housing, and the average price of a single room is now above €480 a month. The demand is there, very much so. What's missing is the right supply: refurbished, well-managed apartments designed for the people who will live in them.

On one side, frozen wealth. On the other, unmet demand. **The market doesn't have a quantity problem — it has a utilisation problem.**

## The Jungle Rent thesis {#jr-thesis}

This is where our model fits in, and not by chance. Jungle Rent does something simple to explain and hard to do well: **we buy underused apartments in Turin's university neighbourhoods, renovate them and put them back to work**.

The mechanics mirror the optimisation logic that the market, at the macro level, still fails to express:

- **September–May**, the apartment houses out-of-town students, with sustainable rent and contracts designed for them.
- **June–August**, when students go home, the same apartment works on the short-term tourist market, in the months when Turin records its peak visitors.

The same square metre, two seasons, two complementary demands. A home that used to be idle goes back to generating value — for those who live in it, for the city, for those who invest.

It's not speculation on resale. It's **active management of a real asset**: you buy something that exists, you improve it, you make it work. That's why the tagline — *Real life Monopoly* — is more than an image. The difference is all in that *real life*: no cardboard squares, but real apartments, real rents, real students and city centres that repopulate instead of emptying out.

## Why timing matters {#timing}

A wave of €2.72 trillion in property in transition isn't background detail: it's the context in which a model like ours takes on strategic meaning. In the coming years an unprecedented quantity of urban apartments will come onto the market — through sale or through heirs' disinterest. Many will end up empty. Some will be sold below value. A few will be **refurbished and put back to work** by those with a method for doing so.

Jungle Rent was built to be among the latter. Not to ride a speculative wave, but to do work the market leaves uncovered: turning frozen stock into housing that people actually need, one apartment at a time, starting from Turin.

## A note on honesty {#honesty}

Big numbers are seductive, and for that very reason they need to be handled with care. €2.72 trillion does not mean €2.72 trillion of opportunity: the vast majority of those homes will stay in the family, off-market, or in areas with no demand. The real value isn't in the breadth of the phenomenon, but in the **ability to select the single right property** — location, price, manageability — and to manage it with discipline.

It's a job done one property at a time. But it's precisely in a market that moves this much that the gap between those who select well and those who buy at random becomes enormous.

The board is tipping. The real game is won by those who know what to do with the squares.
