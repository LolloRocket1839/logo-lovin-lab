# Selling property in Italy: regulatory, tax, and real estate brokerage pitfalls

Buying and selling real estate in Italy represents one of the most complex legal and tax operations a citizen can face. Although it constitutes an apparently natural passage in the life cycle of many, the process is woven with detailed regulations, articulated tax obligations, significant legal risks, and administrative procedures that can easily transform what seems like an ordinary operation into a complex and potentially costly experience.

---

## Fundamental pitfalls in Italian real estate transactions

### Hidden defects and legal protections

One of the most significant risks in real estate transactions concerns **hidden defects** — defects not immediately evident at the time of purchase that compromise the use of the property or significantly reduce its value. Italian law, particularly Article 1490 of the Civil Code, imposes on the seller the fundamental obligation to guarantee that the property sold is free from defects that make it unsuitable for use or appreciably diminish its value.

The buyer has specific protections according to Article 1495 of the Civil Code, which provides for two mandatory and essential deadlines:
- The defect must be reported within **8 days of discovery**
- Legal action must be initiated within **one year of the notarial deed**

The most frequently emerging hidden defects include:
- Water infiltrations from roofs or foundations
- Progressive structural cracks
- Electrical or plumbing systems not compliant with current regulations
- Undeclared presence of asbestos
- Inadequate thermal and acoustic insulation
- Defective windows or unstable floors

For serious structural defects that compromise the stability or habitability of the property, the law provides extended protection: the seller remains liable for a period of **10 years** according to Article 1669 of the Civil Code, provided the defect is reported within one year of discovery.[1]

### Cadastral compliance and urban planning risks

A particularly insidious problem in real estate transactions concerns **cadastral non-compliance**. Italian legislation rigorously requires that the cadastral floor plan of the property corresponds perfectly to the actual state at the time of sale.

If a discrepancy is found, the consequences can be dramatic:
- The notarial deed could be declared null
- Prevention of property transfer
- Invalidation of payment

Similarly, **unresolved building abuses** represent an extremely serious legal and economic risk. If the property has works carried out without the necessary authorizations and not regularized, the sale could be annulled, exposing the seller to criminal, civil, and administrative liability.[1]

### Documentary obligations and energy certification

Italian regulations impose a rigorous series of documentary obligations on the seller. The **Energy Performance Certificate (APE)** is mandatory in both sales and rental deeds.

Its absence exposes the seller to administrative penalties ranging from **€3,000 to €18,000**. Furthermore, the public announcement of a property without energy documentation incurs additional penalties from €500 to €3,000.

The seller is required to provide the buyer with complete and accurate documentation:
- Property provenance deeds
- Urban planning compliance certificates
- Habitability certificate
- Receipts for municipal tax payments
- Documentation relating to any mortgages, seizures, or liens
- Condominium payment statements if the property is in a condominium[2][1]

### Real estate scams and document fraud

The Italian real estate market unfortunately remains vulnerable to various forms of **fraud and scams**. Among the most common are:
- Advertising non-existent properties
- Double sales of the same property
- Falsification of identity and cadastral documents
- Paid lists of unverified properties
- Fake real estate agents extorting money for fictitious visits
- Requests for undue advance payments

A fundamental defense consists of carrying out **cadastral and mortgage searches** through the notary before signing any commitment.[3]

## Tax complexity: registration, cadastral, and mortgage taxes

The taxation of real estate transfers represents one of the most intricate chapters of Italian legislation.

### First home purchase from a private individual
- Registration tax: **2%** (minimum €1,000)
- Mortgage and cadastral taxes: **€50 each**
- Minimum total tax burden: **€1,100**

### Second home purchase
- Registration tax: **9%**
- Mortgage and cadastral taxes: **€50 each**
- IMU with ordinary rate: **0.86%** (can be increased to 1.06%)

If the property is rented, the owner must pay IRPEF on land income, or opt for **Flat Tax** at 21% for free rent contracts or 10% for agreed rent contracts.[2][4]

## The pitfalls of real estate brokerage: when the agency becomes part of the problem

### The question of professional qualification

One of the most significant risks in real estate transactions concerns relying on unqualified intermediaries. In Italy, real estate brokerage is rigorously regulated by **Law 39/1989** and can only be carried out by properly qualified agents registered in the official lists kept by the Chambers of Commerce.

Registration with the **Administrative Economic Repertoire (REA)** of the relevant Chamber of Commerce is not an accessory bureaucratic element, but a fundamental and mandatory legal requirement.[3]

A qualified real estate agent must necessarily:
1. Attend a recognized course
2. Pass a qualifying exam
3. Obtain official registration as a broker at the Chamber of Commerce

Anyone who has not achieved this formal qualification **cannot legally broker properties**. Relying on an unregistered broker leads to serious consequences:
- No regulatory protection
- No traceable professional responsibility
- No insurance guarantee
- Greater exposure to scams or improper behavior

### Conflict of interest in dual brokerage

One of the most insidious problems in real estate practice concerns **conflict of interest in dual brokerage**. According to Article 1754 of the Civil Code, the broker must maintain a position of impartiality and neutrality with respect to the parties involved in the transaction.

However, in practice, it frequently happens that a real estate agency brokers both the seller and the buyer of the same property, collecting a commission from both. Although technically legal, this configuration creates a substantial conflict of interest.[5][6]

### The problem of exclusive mandates

The **exclusive mandate** represents a contractual instrument of great complexity. With this type of assignment, the seller entrusts the management of the property to a single agency.

**Risks of the exclusive mandate:**
- In the case of direct private sale, the seller remains obliged to pay a commission to the agency
- The agency might prioritize "easier" properties to sell
- Exclusivity can prevent collaboration between agencies, reducing property visibility[7]

### The question of commissions and hidden costs

In Italy, commissions required by real estate agencies vary considerably. **The national average ranges between 2% and 4% of the sale price** for each party (seller and buyer).

| Region | Commission |
|--------|------------|
| Piedmont, Liguria, Puglia, Sardinia, Abruzzo, Sicily | 4% |
| Aosta Valley, Basilicata | 5% |
| Trentino, Marche, Veneto, Tuscany | 5% |

An often underestimated element is that agencies can apply **flat-rate fees** on properties with sale prices below €130,000, with flat-rate figures around €4,000 plus VAT.[8]

## Turin's real estate market: dynamics, opportunities, and risks

### The market situation in 2024-2025

Turin today represents one of Italy's most dynamic and evolving real estate markets. In the first quarter of 2025:
- **Property demand** recorded an increase of **32%** compared to the first quarter of the previous year
- **Supply** grew by only **4%**, creating a marked imbalance
- **Transactions** recorded an increase of **12%**[9]

### Price structure by neighborhood

The average transaction price in Turin is around **€2,071/sqm**, but with significant variations:

| Neighborhood | Average price €/sqm |
|--------------|---------------------|
| Historic Center | 3,935 |
| Cavoretto and Gran Madre | 3,078 |
| Crocetta and San Secondo | 2,900 |
| Borgo Vittoria and Parco Dora | 1,400 |
| Aurora, Barriera di Milano, Mirafiori Sud | 1,300-1,400 |

### The structural problem of vacant homes

Turin faces an emerging problem: the accumulation of empty and vacant homes. According to official municipal data, over **22,000 homes** are unused, although alternative sources estimate the real number at over **70,000 units**.

To address this structural housing emergency, the Municipality of Turin is evaluating the **"Vuoti a Rendere"** project, with significant penalties for owners of vacant properties, which could reach **€400 per month** for each vacant property.[9]
