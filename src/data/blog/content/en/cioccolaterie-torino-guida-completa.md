# Turin chocolate shops: complete guide to the world capital of chocolate

Turin is not just the city of the Mole Antonelliana, the automotive industry and Piedmontese culture: it is above all **the world capital of chocolate**, a city where the art of chocolate making has reached unparalleled levels of refinement and tradition. With a history dating back to 1560, when Duke Emanuele Filiberto of Savoy celebrated the transfer of the capital from Chambéry with a steaming cup of hot chocolate, the city has built a unique reputation linked to cocoa processing that continues to shine today.

## The fascinating history of chocolate in Turin

### Royal origins and the Royal Snack

The relationship between Turin and chocolate has its roots in a symbolic gesture of great historical significance. In **1560**, Duke Emanuele Filiberto of Savoy celebrated the transfer of the ducal capital from Chambéry to Turin by symbolically offering a steaming cup of chocolate to the city. This fundamental moment marked the beginning of a "sweet love story" between the Piedmontese capital and the exotic beverage that would forever transform the local gastronomic culture.[1]

From the late sixteenth century onwards, chocolate became a central element of court life. At the Savoy court, the practice of the **Royal Snack** (Merenda Reale) was introduced, a ritual moment of the day designed to break the hunger between lunch (which took place at eleven in the morning) and dinner (set at four). This appointment, which involved the consumption of chocolate, soon became an established custom in Turin society.[2]

### The eighteenth century: the invention of Bicerin

The eighteenth century represented a crucial moment in the history of Turin chocolate. It was during this period that the **Bicerin** was born, a beverage that is still considered one of Turin's gastronomic excellences. According to tradition, the Bicerin was created by the eponymous café located in Piazza della Consolata in **1763**.[3]

The Bicerin is an extraordinarily refined beverage: three perfectly balanced layers of ingredients—coffee, melted chocolate, and cream—served in tall glasses or crystal goblets. In 2001, it was officially recognized by the Piedmont Region as a **traditional Piedmontese food product**. The beverage has fascinated illustrious figures such as Count Camillo Benso di Cavour, Pablo Picasso, Alexandre Dumas, Ernest Hemingway (who included it among "the hundred things in the world he would save") and Umberto Eco.[3]

### The nineteenth century: the industrial revolution and the birth of Gianduiotto

The nineteenth century represented the century of transformation and creative explosion in Turin chocolate making. In **1826**, Pier Paul Caffarel opened the first true industrial chocolate laboratory in Turin, located on Via Balbis in the San Donato district. Caffarel purchased a revolutionary industrial machine capable of producing over 320 kilograms of chocolate per day.[4][5][6]

The decisive moment came in **1852**, when the Caffarel house began experimenting with combining cocoa with the precious Tonda Gentile hazelnuts from the Langhe. In **1865**, precisely during the Turin Carnival, Prochet and Caffarel officially presented the **Gianduiotto** to the public. The presentation was made by the famous Turin mask Gianduja, who distributed the new delicacy to revelers in the city streets.[7][8]

## The great historic companies

### Caffarel: from foundation to the creation of Gianduiotto

Caffarel represents the fundamental pillar of Turin's chocolate history. Founded in **1826** by Pier Paul Caffarel, the company was among the very first European laboratories to transform artisanal cocoa processing into true industrial production. In 1997, Caffarel became part of the Lindt & Sprüngli group. The company continues to maintain its historic laboratory and dedication to creating extraordinary quality chocolate, using the **Nocciola Piemonte IGP** as a fundamental ingredient.[4][5]

### Venchi: from confectioner worker to multinational company

The story of **Venchi** represents a fascinating example of economic transformation. It all began in **1878** in Borgo Vanchiglia, when **Silviano Venchi** decided to open his first artisanal workshop. In 1890, Venchi launched the first boxes of cocoa powder with the famous "Due Vecchi" (Two Old Men) logo. Between 1926 and 1928, Venchi completed work on its historic factory at Corso Francia 325, which employed nearly three thousand workers.[9]

### Baratti & Milano: the invention of Cremino

The history of **Baratti & Milano** embodies the cultural richness and elegance of bourgeois Turin. Founded in **1875**, the famous café-confectionery is located in the magnificent **Galleria Subalpina**. It was Ferdinando Baratti who invented the **Cremino**, characterized by its layered structure: two outer layers of gianduja chocolate and an inner layer of hazelnut chocolate paste.[10][11][12]

### Peyrano: supplier to the Royal House

The **Peyrano Chocolate Shop** represents one of the oldest companies in the sector, founded in **1915**. Peyrano's reputation grew notably after World War I, when the company began supplying chocolate to the **Royal House of Savoy**.[10][14][15]

## Contemporary excellences

### Guido Gobino: sixty years of craftsmanship

The **Guido Gobino Artisanal Chocolate Shop** represents a contemporary excellence that blends tradition and innovation. The story begins in **1946**, when the laboratory was founded. In **1995**, Guido Gobino conceived the **Tourinot**, an elegant reinterpretation of the Gianduiotto. In **2024**, the company celebrated sixty years of activity. The Salt Cremino was named "best praline in the world" by the London Academy of Chocolate in 2008.[17][18][19][20]

### Stratta: Turin's oldest pastry shop

**Stratta** is Turin's oldest pastry shop, founded in **1836** by master confectioners Stratta and Reina in the splendid setting of Piazza San Carlo. The venue has been declared a **Historic Venue of Italy**.[21][22][23]

### Pfatisch: Bavarian excellence

The **Pfatisch Chocolate Shop** was opened in **1915** by Bavarian master **Gustavo Pfatisch** on Via Sacchi. It can boast the title of "Suppliers to the Royal House". Writers such as **Cesare Pavese**, **Primo Levi**, **Mario Soldati** and **Norberto Bobbio** have sat at the tables of this historic chocolate shop. It received the **Best Savory Pastry 2023** award from Gambero Rosso.[14][24]

### Guido Castagna and La Perla di Torino

**Guido Castagna**, opened in **2002**, has won fame in just a few years. **La Perla di Torino**, founded by Sergio Arzilli in 1992, is an interpreter of refined chocolate art with a production of over 50 recipes, all strictly **gluten-free**.[15][16][25]

## Turin's sweet specialties

### Gianduiotto: the king of Turin chocolate

The **Gianduiotto** represents the apotheosis of Turin's chocolate tradition, a triangular prism-shaped chocolate made with gianduja chocolate. It is obtained by kneading bitter cocoa and sugar with the famous **Piedmont Tonda Gentile Hazelnut**. The Gianduiotto is included among **traditional Piedmontese food products** and is a candidate for protected geographical indication.[1][7]

### Cremino: the refinement of layers

The **Cremino** is a square or rectangular chocolate, structured in **three layers**: two outer layers of gianduja chocolate and an inner layer of "Tonda Gentile" quality hazelnut cream.[11][12][26]

### Bicerin: the beverage of literati

The **Bicerin** combines coffee, melted chocolate and cream in three perfect layers. It is inextricably linked to Turin's literature and culture.[3]

### Boero: the cherry in chocolate

The **Boero** is a classic with a chocolate shell and a heart of liqueur cream, often with a **whole cherry** submerged in liqueur. Preparation requires extraordinary meticulousness.[27][28]

### Other specialties

The Turin tradition also includes the **Diablottino**, the **Giacometta** (whose shape recalls Gianduja's hat), the **Grappino** and the **Alpino** (chocolate filled with liqueur cream).[11][26]

## CioccolaTò: the great chocolate festival

**CioccolaTò** is the artisanal chocolate *kermesse* held every year in Turin since **2003**.

### CioccolaTò 2025: February 27 - March 2

The 2025 edition will take place from **February 27 to March 2** in the splendid setting of **Piazza Vittorio Veneto**. The event will also involve the National Cinema Museum, the Circolo dei Lettori, the Massimo Cinema and the Gallerie d'Italia. About 50 producers will occupy the exhibition area. On the last Sunday, the famous **"Gianduiotto Hunt"** takes place.[31][32]

### CioccolaTò 2026: February 13-17

The 2026 edition will be held from **February 13 to 17**, coinciding with **Valentine's Day** and **Carnival**.[33][34]

## Piedmont IGP Hazelnut: the magic ingredient

The **Piedmont IGP Hazelnut**, particularly the **Tonda Gentile Trilobata** variety, is a fundamental ingredient for all gianduja art. This hazelnut has gained worldwide reputation for its extraordinary quality, unique taste and culinary versatility.[35]

---

## Ferrero and Nutella: Alba's contribution

Although Ferrero is headquartered in **Alba**, its contribution to Piedmont's chocolate heritage deserves mention. In **1946**, **Pietro Ferrero** invented what would become the world's most famous spreadable cream: **Nutella**. The **first jar of Nutella** was produced on **April 20, 1964** at the Ferrero factory in Alba.[29][30]

## Conclusion

Turin represents an essential destination for any chocolate lover, a place where history, tradition and innovation meet in extraordinary harmony. From large companies like Caffarel and Venchi, to artisanal boutiques like Guido Gobino and Guido Castagna, from historic institutions like Stratta, Baratti & Milano and Pfatisch, the city offers an incredibly rich panorama of chocolate experiences.
